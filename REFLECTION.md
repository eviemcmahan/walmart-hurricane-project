## 1. Data Quality Impact

> *Describe a specific data quality issue you encountered in the supplied data. How did your cleaning decision change the outcome of your analysis? What would have happened if you had not caught it?*

The data quality issue that changed our results the most was Strawberry Pop-Tarts being split across four SKUs (PT-12, POPTART-STRAW, PT_STRAWBERRY and SKU#459812), each with its own product name. The SKU column looked clean in the profile with 45 distinct values, and every one matched the catalog. The problem only showed up when we compared it to the 38 product names in the catalog and the 62 in the transactions. The code found 14 groups of SKUs that were the same product. We matched the rest by hand, including all four Pop-Tarts SKUs, and built a mapping table that took 45 SKUs down to 20 products. This changed the main finding of the analysis. In the fragmented view, the biggest Pop-Tarts SKU showed 1,385 units, but the real total was 5,218, which is 3.8 times larger. During the surge window, the top Pop-Tarts SKU (PT-12, 103 units a day) ranked fourth, behind three bottled water SKUs. After consolidating, Pop-Tarts was the number one product at 378 units a day, ahead of water at 321. If we had not caught this, we would have told Walmart that water was the top storm item and that Pop-Tarts were four small products that each peaked under 240 units a day, when they actually peaked at 682 units on September 25. Q1 would also have been wrong, because Breakfast’s +414% surge only shows up correctly once the SKUs are combined. That would mean ordering too little of the product customers wanted most before the storm. A second issue had a similar risk, where the store file listed Orlando and Kissimmee twice under different store_id spellings. Without normalizing the IDs and dropping the duplicates, every sale from those two stores would have been counted twice when we joined transactions to stores for Q4.

## 2. ETL Trade-offs

> *You made choices about how to standardize SKUs, handle missing timestamps, and join weather data. Pick one decision and explain: what alternative approach could you have taken, and how might it have changed your results?*

One decision that we made was to flip the 130 negative quantity rows to positive, which together represented -241 total units. After flipping these quantities, total units changed from 49,343 to 49,825. We decided to flip the quantities because none of the negative rows had a matching positive row with the same transaction_id, which would have indicated a return, and the values were all small, so we assumed them to be sign typos. The alternative approaches could have been to drop the 130 rows or leave them as negative. If we dropped these rows, it would have removed actual sales from both the store and category totals, decreasing both counts. On the other hand, keeping them as negative would have decreased the baseline and surge averages that we used, impacting our surge multiples. The direction of change would depend on whether the baseline or surge windows had more negative quantities, and therefore lost a larger share of units. For example, if more negative quantities were in the baseline time frame, then leaving them as negative would have made the surge ratio slightly higher than if we flipped them to positive. However, these alternative decisions wouldn’t have changed our surge multiples by a large amount, since only 130 out of 25,532 rows were negative.

## 3. Pipeline Trust

> *The Walmart CDO argued that the biggest risk is not the model — it is the pipeline. Based on your experience in this project, do you agree or disagree? What was the most fragile part of your data pipeline?*

We agree with the CDO that the pipeline is the biggest risk. The analysis we did for Q1 to Q5 was pretty simple (averages, percent changes and correlations), and most of our time went into cleaning and preparing the data. Our answers changed a lot more because of cleaning decisions than because of the way we analyzed the data. We think the most fragile part of our pipeline was the SKU mapping table. The code only found 14 of the product groups, so we had to make the rest of the calls ourselves by looking at the catalog. Some of these were judgment calls, like merging the Roku Streaming Stick (STREAM-01) with the Streaming Device (ROKU-STD) and keeping Monopoly and playing cards separate. Our checks make sure the mapping doesn’t add or remove rows or change the unit total, but they would still pass if we had merged two products that shouldn’t be merged. So a mistake wouldn’t cause an error, it would just give us the wrong answer. The table also has to be updated by hand. If a new SKU showed up in the next storm’s data, the code would print it as unmapped and keep going, so the products could get split up again without anything stopping. The weather join was also fragile, because it only works if the sales and the weather are both on Eastern dates. Without the timezone conversion, 8 of every 48 hourly readings would land on the wrong day, which could have changed the 2 day lag we found in Q3. To make the pipeline easier to trust, we added asserts on the row counts, validate= on every join, a check that clean_all gives the same result as running each step one at a time, and a decision log (32 entries), so anyone can rerun the notebook and see what changed and why.

## 4. Business vs. Data

> *For Question 5 (board games), your team had to make a recommendation with imperfect data. How did you weigh statistical evidence against business risk? Would you have decided differently with more time or more data?*

Our statistical evidence supported that the board game sales increase was a real signal rather than an artifact, although it was smaller than the +250% increase that the question originally suggested. Entertainment sales did peak around +250%, but only on the two days before landfall. On Sept 24 and 25, Entertainment was up 232% in the transactions and 267% in the database, but over the full 7 day surge window it was only up 126% and 145%. Entertainment also ranked only 5th of 10 categories on the peak days, which made us conclude that the surge was a normal storm peak rather than a signal special to board games. We checked different ways that the signal could be an artifact, including looking at whether the surge appears in both the transaction file and the inventory database, whether there was category lumping, if other categories also peaked, the number of purchases the surge comes from, if there were reporting lags, and any SKU miscodings. All of this evidence pointed to the fact that there was a true surge in entertainment sales, but it was not stronger than other categories. We weighed this evidence against business risk by recommending a modest increase, about 2.6 times normal Monopoly and 2.0 times normal Playing Cards, and prioritized the stocking behind water, batteries, flashlights, and Pop-Tarts, which are more essential items in a storm, or in Pop-Tarts’ case, surged more. One business risk was that our analysis was only based on data from one storm. We also did not have data on the cost or margins of any products, so we were not able to make decisions on how much an oversupply would cost the store. With more time or data, we would want to analyze several storms to see whether games rise every time, as well as cost data to determine the order size.
